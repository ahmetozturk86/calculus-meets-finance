How we found your plan

The math behind the plan, step by step. Open any part.

Why mixing helps each asset alone vs your mix, same risk

Typical growth per year that each choice can reach without breaking your bad-year limit. With only one asset next to the savings account, the risk limit stops you early. Mixing assets that don't fall together lets you grow faster for the same risk.

What could happen next year the bell curve

The curve shows where your money can be after one year with your mix. The red tail is the worst years (the worst 1 in 20 at 95%). Where the tail starts is the Value-at-Risk, your bad-year loss. It must stay right of your limit (dashed line).

How the answer is found Intermediate Value Theorem + bisection

Any fixed mix of stocks, gold and bonds behaves like one fund. Put a share f of your money in that fund and keep the rest in the savings account. The bad-year loss VaR(f) grows with f without jumps. At f = 0 it is below your limit and at the top it is above, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem it crosses your limit somewhere. Bisection finds that point by halving the interval again and again.

What if rare crashes happen? fat tails

The bell curve says a really bad year is almost impossible, but real markets sometimes crash. Here the stock's average return stays the same, but now and then it suddenly falls. The crash-aware VaR still grows with f without jumps, so the same Intermediate Value Theorem + bisection finds the new answer. Shown for stocks + savings account (Module 4b).
