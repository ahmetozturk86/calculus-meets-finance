Watch steps turn into smooth formulas

Each section has one big slider. Move it and watch a step-by-step model turn into a smooth formula: the limit. The small sliders change the problem itself.

1. Compounding interest: stairs become a smooth curve

The bank adds interest n times a year, so your money grows in steps. If interest is added every instant, money grows on the smooth curve P·ert.

Look at: with yearly interest the stairs are big and end well below the curve. Move to monthly, then daily: the steps get tiny and the stairs lie on the curve. That is the limit (1 + r/n)nt → ert.

2. Option price: tree bars become a bell curve

The binomial tree lets the stock go only up or down, N times. The bars show where the stock can end and how likely each place is in the pricing world, where the stock grows like the bank (rate r, not its real return): the risk-neutral probabilities. The option pays ST − K only to the right of K (green). Its price is that payoff averaged over the bars and then discounted to today (times e−rT). Black–Scholes uses the smooth curve instead.

Stock S = 100, rate r = 5%, T = 1 year.

Look at: with N = 1 or 2 there are only a few fat bars. Slide N up: the bars get thinner and fill the curve, so the tree price becomes the Black–Scholes price.

3. Random walk: zigzags become a stock chart

A coin-flip model of a stock: every step the price goes up or down by the same small amount (μ = 10%, σ = 20% a year). The top chart shows 20 possible years. The bottom chart shows, for every price, the chance the stock ends the year below it: stairs for the walk, a smooth S-curve for its limit, geometric Brownian motion (GBM).

Look at: with one step a year every path is a single straight line, up or down, so the year can end at only two prices. With daily steps the paths look like real stock charts and the stairs lie on the S-curve. The biggest gap (orange, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance) shrinks like 1/√n.

4. How much to put in the stock?

You put a share f of your money in a stock (return μ = 10%, risk σ = 20%) and the rest in the bank (3%); f above 1 means borrowing. The rates are continuous, as in section 1: μ = 10% is an average gain of e0.1 − 1 = 10.5% a year. The bell shows where your money can be after one year. The red tail is the worst 5% of years; where it starts is the Value-at-Risk. Rule: VaR must not go past the loss limit L (dashed line).

Finding the largest safe f

Below, every f is one point: its VaR. At small f the VaR is under L, at f = 2 it is over L, and the line has no jumps, so it must cross L (Intermediate Value Theorem). Bisection finds the crossing by halving [0, 2] again and again.

4b. What if rare crashes happen?

The bell curve says a really bad year is almost impossible, but real markets sometimes crash. Here the stock now and then falls suddenly, while its average return stays the same. The chart shows a year at f = , the answer of section 4: the curve with crashes next to the plain bell (gray).

Loss limit L = 15%, the worst 5% of years.

Look at: with crashes the left side of the curve gets fatter (a “fat tail”), and the red tail reaches further, past the limit. The crash-aware VaR still grows with f without jumps, so the Intermediate Value Theorem and bisection still find the largest safe f, and every VaR on the way has no formula and is itself found by bisection.

5. The best mix of stocks, gold and bonds

Any fixed mix of stocks, gold and bonds behaves like one fund. For each mix, bisection (section 4) finds how much of your money it may hold under the 15% loss limit, which gives its growth. The hill shows that growth for every share of bonds; the rest of the mix is stocks.

No borrowing: at most all of your money goes into the mix, the rest stays in the bank (3%).

A finer and finer search: the best mix as a limit

Each dot is one mix of stocks, gold and bonds (the corners are “all stocks”, “all gold” and “all bonds”). The stronger the blue, the faster the mix grows under the 15% limit. The computer tries every dot and keeps the fastest. With a smaller step there are more dots, so the search gets closer to the true best mix: the limit as the step goes to 0.

Look at: while the step is big, the green dot jumps around, but the growth it finds only climbs toward the true best. Near the top many dots are almost equally strong: about 73% stocks is what matters, the split between gold and bonds barely does.
