Each section has one big slider. Move it and watch a step-by-step model turn into a smooth formula (the limit). The small sliders change the problem itself.

1. Compounding interest: stairs become a smooth curve

The bank adds interest n times a year, so your money grows in steps (blue stairs). If interest is added every instant, money grows on the smooth red curve P·ert.

Look at: with yearly interest the stairs are big and end well below the red curve. Move to monthly, then daily: the steps get tiny and the stairs lie on the curve. That is the limit (1 + r/n)nt → ert.

2. Option price: tree bars become a bell curve

The binomial tree lets the stock go only up or down, N times. The blue bars show where the stock can end and how likely each place is. The option pays ST − K only to the right of K (green). Its price is that payoff averaged over the bars. Black–Scholes uses the smooth red curve instead.

(stock S = 100, rate r = 0.05, T = 1 year)
Look at: with N = 1 or 2 there are only a few fat bars. Slide N up: the bars get thinner and fill the red curve exactly, so the tree price becomes the Black–Scholes price. (This is also Module 3: a random walk of up/down steps becomes the smooth GBM.)

4. How much to put in the stock?

You put a fraction f of your money in a stock (return μ = 10%, risk σ = 20%) and the rest in the bank (3%). The bell shows where your money can be after one year. The red tail is the worst 5% of years; where it starts is the Value-at-Risk. Rule: VaR must not go past the loss limit L (black dashed line).

Finding the largest safe f

Below, every f is one point: its VaR. At small f the VaR is under L, at f = 2 it is over L, and the line has no jumps, so it must cross L (Intermediate Value Theorem). Bisection finds the crossing by halving [0, 2] again and again.
